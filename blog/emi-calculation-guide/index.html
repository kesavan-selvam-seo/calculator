An EMI is your regular monthly loan repayment. The amount depends on how much you borrow, the interest rate, and how long you take to repay. A ₹5 lakh loan at 12% for five years works out to ₹11,122.22 per month under a standard reducing-balance formula. The real decision is about both the monthly payment and the total cost.

What is EMI in a loan?

EMI stands for Equated Monthly Instalment. It is a scheduled monthly payment used to repay an instalment loan. With a conventional, constant-rate reducing-balance loan, the payment remains the same each month but the split between principal and interest changes over time.

At the beginning of repayment, interest is calculated on a relatively large outstanding principal, so more of the EMI goes towards interest. As you repay the principal, the outstanding balance falls; interest reduces and a greater portion of the same EMI repays the principal. This process is called amortization.

In brief: EMI is not the same as interest alone. A regular EMI pays both interest and part of the loan amount. Before choosing a loan, compare monthly affordability and total repayment.

EMI calculation formula explained

For a loan with a fixed monthly interest rate and equal repayments over a defined number of months, the standard reducing-balance EMI formula is:

Monthly loan EMI
EMI = [P × r × (1 + r)n] ÷ [(1 + r)n − 1]
Equivalent expression: EMI = P × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)−n]. If r = 0, EMI = P ÷ n.
PPrincipal: the original amount financed.
rMonthly interest rate as a decimal.
nTotal number of monthly instalments.
EMIFixed monthly payment, before separately charged fees.

Convert an annual interest rate into a monthly rate

When the quoted annual rate is a nominal annual rate with monthly application, use r = annual rate (%) ÷ (12 × 100). For example, 12% annually gives r = 12 ÷ 1,200 = 0.01, or 1% per month. For a 5-year loan, n = 5 × 12 = 60 payments.

Real loan contracts can use daily reducing balances, a different compounding convention, payment timing or disbursement schedule. This standard formula is a useful estimate, but your lender's repayment schedule is the authoritative account calculation.

Step-by-step EMI example: ₹5 lakh at 12% for 5 years

Suppose you borrow ₹5,00,000 at a nominal annual interest rate of 12%, repayable monthly over five years. Assume monthly reducing-balance interest, a constant rate and no fees or prepayments.

  1. Principal (P): ₹5,00,000.
  2. Monthly rate (r): 12% ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.01.
  3. Number of payments (n): 5 × 12 = 60.
  4. Substitute: EMI = 5,00,000 × 0.01 × (1.01)60 ÷ [(1.01)60 − 1].
  5. Result: monthly EMI ≈ ₹11,122.22.
  6. Total repayment: unrounded EMI × 60 ≈ ₹6,67,333.43; total interest ≈ ₹1,67,333.43.

How much of each EMI goes to principal and interest?

With a monthly reducing-balance loan, interest each month is based on the opening outstanding balance. Principal repaid in that month equals EMI minus the month's interest. The closing balance is the opening balance minus the principal repaid.

Monthly amortization working
Interest = Opening balance × r
Principal paid = EMI − Interest
Closing balance = Opening balance − Principal paid
The interest/principal division gradually shifts as the loan balance declines, if the rate and EMI remain unchanged.

How do interest rate and loan tenure affect EMI?

For the same principal, a higher interest rate increases the cost of borrowing. A longer repayment duration generally makes monthly payments easier to manage, but you pay interest for more months. Flat interest rate vs reducing-balance interest

A reducing-balance loan calculates interest based on the principal still outstanding. As the loan is repaid, its interest component gradually reduces. Most online EMI formula examples describe this model.

With a flat-interest loan, the advertised interest amount is commonly calculated on the original principal for the entire tenure, even as repayments are made. That means a 12% flat annual rate is not financially equivalent to a 12% reducing-balance annual rate.

Reducing balance example

₹5 lakh at 12% nominal annual interest for 5 years gives an EMI of approximately ₹11,122.22 and interest of ₹1,67,333.43.

Flat interest illustration

₹5 lakh × 12% × 5 years = ₹3 lakh flat interest. Total repayment is ₹8 lakh, or about ₹13,333.33 across 60 equal payments.

Always confirm the interest method and effective annual borrowing cost with your lender. Compare the lender's APR (Annual Percentage Rate) or equivalent all-in cost disclosure where applicable, because interest alone may not represent every loan fee.

Fixed vs floating loan EMI

A fixed-rate loan uses a specified interest rate for the agreed fixed period. If all other terms stay the same, an equal monthly EMI is predictable. A floating-rate loan can have interest resets linked to a benchmark and lender spread; rate changes may affect EMI, tenure or both under the applicable terms.

The Reserve Bank of India's guidance on resetting floating-rate EMI-based personal loans addresses disclosure and available borrower choices when rates change, within its scope. RBI also explains these requirements in its official FAQ. Check which provisions apply to your product and lender.

Important: a floating-rate loan should not be forecast for its entire lifetime assuming the interest rate will never change. Use your current rate for an illustration, and test how a higher rate or extended tenure would change repayments.

Does EMI include processing fees, GST and insurance?

The standard EMI formula covers principal and interest on the financed amount. It does not automatically include fees paid separately, taxes on eligible services, insurance premiums, late-payment charges or other expenses. If a fee or premium is added to the loan principal, that financed amount can itself attract interest.

Compare the full disbursed amount, total principal financed and all disclosed charges before signing. RBI's fair-lending and Key Facts Statement (KFS) guidance discusses borrower disclosures, including charges and loan terms. Where a KFS and APR apply, review them alongside the EMI schedule, rather than relying only on an advertised headline rate.

How can you reduce EMI or total loan interest?

There are different ways to make a loan easier to repay, but they have different effects on the total cost. Reducing your EMI is not always the same as reducing your total interest paid.

  • Borrow only what you need: reducing the financed principal reduces both the EMI and future interest at the same rate and tenure.
  • Compare all-in borrowing costs: review rate, fees and applicable APR—not just a promotional interest rate.
  • Choose an appropriate tenure: a shorter tenure may save substantial interest if the monthly payment remains affordable.
  • Consider a part-prepayment: an early principal reduction can cut future interest; ask how the lender applies it to EMI or tenure.
  • Review balance transfers carefully: compare the expected interest saving with transfer fees, new conditions and remaining term.
  • Check rate-reset and prepayment terms: rules can depend on the type of loan, borrower, lender and applicable directions.

For floating-rate EMI-based personal loans within the RBI guidance's scope, borrowers should receive information about changes and options such as EMI or tenure adjustment and prepayment, subject to applicable rules. Do not assume the same fees or pre-closure conditions apply to every loan.

EMI calculations for different loan types

The same reducing-balance mathematical principle can be used for many instalment loans, but product-specific treatment of rates, moratoriums, insurance, disbursements and fees may differ. Always confirm the agreement and repayment schedule.

Common EMI calculation mistakes to avoid

  • Using 12 instead of 0.01: enter the monthly interest rate as a decimal in the formula, not as a whole-number percentage.
  • Entering years as the number of months: a five-year monthly loan usually has 60 scheduled instalments, not five.
  • Comparing flat and reducing rates directly: identical quoted percentages can produce different effective borrowing costs.
  • Ignoring charges: EMI alone may exclude fees, taxes, insurance or the effects of disbursement timing.
  • Assuming a floating rate cannot change: rate resets may alter loan costs and repayment schedules.
  • Rounding too early: keep full numerical precision during calculation and round only final displayed amounts.
  • Focusing only on a low EMI: longer repayment periods can significantly increase lifetime interest.

Frequently asked questions about EMI

How do you calculate EMI?

Use EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ [(1 + r)n − 1] for a standard reducing-balance instalment loan. P is loan principal, r is the monthly interest rate as a decimal, and n is the number of monthly payments.

What is the EMI for a ₹5 lakh loan at 12% for 5 years?

The EMI is approximately ₹11,122.22 per month over 60 months. Total interest is about ₹1,67,333.43, excluding separately charged fees and assuming a fixed 12% nominal annual rate applied monthly.

Does increasing tenure reduce EMI?

Generally yes, when principal and interest rate are unchanged. However, paying over more months usually increases the total interest cost.

Are flat and reducing-balance EMI calculations the same?

No. The reducing-balance formula charges interest on the outstanding principal. A flat-interest method calculates charges on the original principal, which can make identical advertised rate percentages misleading.

Can a floating-rate loan's EMI increase?

Yes. Depending on the contract and applicable regulation, a rate reset may change EMI, tenure or both. Review your lender's disclosures and repayment statements.

Does EMI include processing fees and insurance?

Not by default. These items may be charged separately or, if financed, added to principal. Review the complete loan cost disclosure.

What happens if I make a part-prepayment?

Paying down principal earlier can save future interest. The resulting EMI or remaining term depends on the lender's recalculation method and loan agreement.

How is first-month EMI interest calculated?

Under a standard monthly reducing-balance model, it equals the opening principal multiplied by the monthly interest rate. EMI minus that interest reduces the outstanding principal.
