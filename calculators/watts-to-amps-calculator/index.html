What is a watts to amps calculator?

A watts to amps calculator estimates electric current using real electrical power and voltage. Watts (W) measure real power, amperes (A) measure current, and volts (V) measure electric potential difference. All three quantities are related, but AC supplies can require another input called power factor.

This calculator lets you select DC, single-phase AC or a balanced three-phase AC system. It also offers the reverse conversion from amperes to watts, so you can calculate real electrical power from current, voltage and power factor in the same tool.

Watts to amps formulas: DC, single-phase and three-phase

DC circuit

Current (A) = Watts ÷ Volts

I = P ÷ V. Applies to electrical DC input power.

Single-phase AC

Current (A) = Watts ÷ (RMS V × PF)

I = P ÷ (V × PF), using true power factor.

Balanced three-phase AC

Line current (A) = Watts ÷ (√3 × VLL × PF)

Uses total 3-phase real power and RMS line-to-line voltage.

To calculate current from a power value in kilowatts (kW), multiply by 1,000 to obtain watts first. When working with AC circuits, power factor must be greater than zero and no more than 1 for a positive real-power calculation. A balanced three-phase circuit uses RMS line-to-line voltage in the displayed √3 formula, not line-to-neutral voltage.

For a technical explanation of the relationship between real power, apparent power, voltage and current, see this three-phase electrical power formula guide. Remember that equipment data sheets and nameplates can distinguish electrical input power from mechanical output power.

How to convert watts to amps

  1. Select Watts → Amps at the top of the calculator.
  2. Choose DC, Single-phase AC or Three-phase AC (balanced).
  3. Enter the real electrical input power in watts or kilowatts.
  4. Enter DC voltage, single-phase RMS voltage or three-phase RMS line-to-line voltage as labelled.
  5. For AC, enter a known power factor between 0 and 1. Do not assume a device's PF is 1 unless appropriate.
  6. Calculate to see current in amperes and milliamperes, plus formula steps and AC apparent power where applicable.

Example 1: 100 watts to amps at 230 V DC

Consider a DC load that consumes 100 watts from a 230-volt DC supply. Dividing 100 by 230 gives approximately 0.4348 amperes, or 434.8 milliamperes. The exact displayed result depends on your selected decimal precision.

Although 230 volts is often discussed in household AC settings, this is explicitly a DC example. For a 230 V AC load, the result depends on the true power factor of the device.

Example 2: 1 kW to amps at 230 V single-phase AC

Suppose an electrical load consumes 1,000 W from a 230 V single-phase AC supply with power factor 0.90. The current is 1,000 ÷ (230 × 0.90), which equals approximately 4.8309 A. With unity PF, the same real power would correspond to only about 4.3478 A.

This shows why simply dividing watts by volts can underestimate AC current when power factor is below one. A power factor is a ratio, not an efficiency percentage, and it may change with the equipment's operating conditions.

Example 3: 5 kW to amps at 415 V three-phase AC

For a balanced three-phase load consuming 5 kW total real power at 415 V line-to-line and PF 0.85, calculate I = 5,000 ÷ (√3 × 415 × 0.85). The result is approximately 8.18 A of line current under the stated steady-state assumptions.

The three-phase formula uses the total load's real electrical input power. If a motor label lists mechanical shaft-output power, its electrical input power may be higher because of efficiency losses. Additional efficiency data would be needed before estimating input current.

Amps to watts: reverse conversion

When current is already known, select the Amps → Watts tab. The reverse DC formula is P = V × I, while single-phase AC is P = V × I × PF. Balanced three-phase real power is P = √3 × VLL × IL × PF.

For example, 10 A from a 230 V single-phase AC supply with PF 0.90 represents approximately 2,070 W of real electrical power, or 2.07 kW. The calculator also reports apparent power in volt-amperes for AC circuits, which can differ from watts.

Watts vs amps vs volts vs power factor

Watts (W)

Measure real electrical power. A kilowatt is 1,000 watts; kW is not the same as kWh, which measures energy.

Amperes (A)

Measure electric current. One ampere equals 1,000 milliamperes (mA).

Power factor (PF)

In AC circuits, true PF is the ratio of real power (W) to apparent power (VA). A lower PF means more current for the same real power and voltage.

Power factor is used for the AC conversion modes but not for a steady DC circuit. For three-phase AC, the chosen formula assumes balanced operation. An unbalanced system may require measuring and analyzing the individual phases rather than relying on a single aggregate power-factor figure.

Watts, kW, VA and kVA: why the units differ

Watts and kilowatts describe real power. Volt-amperes (VA) and kilovolt-amperes (kVA) describe apparent AC power. With single-phase AC, apparent power equals RMS voltage × RMS current. With balanced three-phase AC, apparent power equals √3 × line-to-line RMS voltage × RMS line current.

For example, 1,000 W at 0.8 PF requires 1,250 VA of apparent power. The ratio matters when reading AC equipment ratings, but this calculator does not automatically size generators, wiring, power supplies or protection devices.

Electrical safety and calculator limitations

The output estimates steady-state RMS current using idealized formulas. Real systems can have harmonics, changing PF, phase imbalance, duty cycle variation, motor-starting current, transient surges and losses. Nameplate rated current may not match a calculation made from nominal wattage and supply voltage.

Do not use this result by itself to select a circuit breaker, fuse, wire gauge, socket or electrical installation. Electrical equipment can present shock and fire hazards, and appropriate protection depends on local electrical codes, conductor characteristics, installation conditions and professional judgment. Consult a qualified electrician or engineer for equipment design and installation.

Common mistakes when converting watts to amps

  • Ignoring power factor: single-phase AC and three-phase AC load current can be higher than watts ÷ volts when PF is below 1.
  • Mixing kW with W: convert 5 kW to 5,000 W before applying a formula written in watts.
  • Using the wrong three-phase voltage: the √3 formula here uses line-to-line RMS voltage, not line-to-neutral voltage.
  • Using mechanical output power as electrical input: motor shaft-rated power may require efficiency to estimate real electrical input power.
  • Confusing VA with W: apparent power and real power are equal in magnitude only at unity power factor for a standard AC load.
  • Using steady-state current as a protective-device rating: inrush, continuous-load rules and local electrical standards must also be considered.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many amps are 100 watts at 230 volts?

For DC or an ideal resistive single-phase AC circuit at unity power factor, 100 ÷ 230 is about 0.4348 amperes. AC current can be higher when PF is below one.

How do I calculate amps from watts?

Use watts ÷ volts for DC. For single-phase AC, divide by RMS volts × PF. For balanced three-phase AC, divide by √3 × line-to-line RMS volts × PF.

What is the single-phase AC watts-to-amps formula?

I = P ÷ (V × PF), where P is real power, V is RMS voltage and PF is true power factor.

How do I calculate amps from three-phase kilowatts?

Multiply the kW by 1,000, then divide by √3 × RMS line-to-line voltage × PF for a balanced three-phase system.

Can I convert watts to amps without voltage?

No. The current also depends on the voltage and AC power factor, so wattage alone is insufficient.

What is the difference between watts and volt-amperes?

Watts measure real electrical power and volt-amperes measure apparent AC power. PF = W ÷ VA.

Can this calculator determine wire or circuit breaker size?

No. The result is an illustrative steady-state calculation, not an equipment rating or an electrical-installation design. Consult a qualified electrician and applicable electrical codes.
