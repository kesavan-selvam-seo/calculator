Net run rate formula

Net run rate measures how quickly a cricket team scores compared with how quickly its opponents score against it. To calculate tournament NRR, combine the runs and overs from every qualifying match before dividing. Averaging individual match NRR values is not the same calculation when match lengths differ.

NRR = (Total runs scored ÷ Total overs faced) − (Total runs conceded ÷ Total overs bowled)

Because overs are recorded as overs and balls, the calculator converts each innings to legal balls first. It then computes both rates in runs per six-ball over. A positive NRR indicates a faster scoring rate than conceding rate; a negative NRR indicates the reverse.

Worked example: a two-match T20 tournament

Suppose your team makes 180 in 20 overs and concedes 160 in 20 overs in the opening match. In the second match, the team scores 140 all out after 18 overs and 4 balls, while conceding 150 in 20 overs. The all-out innings is counted as 20 overs for NRR. The team therefore totals 320 runs in 40 effective overs faced and concedes 310 runs in 40 overs bowled.

NRR = (320 ÷ 40) − (310 ÷ 40) = +0.250

Three cricket NRR rules people often miss

1. 18.4 overs is not 18.4 decimal overs

The digit after the dot counts legal balls. Thus 18.4 overs equals 18 + 4/6 = approximately 18.6667 overs. An entry such as 18.6 is invalid; six more legal balls means 19.0 overs.

2. All out means using the entire entitled quota

If a side is dismissed at 140 in 17.3 overs in a standard 20-over match, that innings is treated as 20 overs for NRR, not 17.5 decimal overs. Tick the appropriate All out? box in the calculator.

3. Tournament NRR uses aggregated totals

When matches have different innings lengths, their individual run rates carry different amounts of playing time. Add all scored runs and effective overs together, do the same for conceded runs and overs, and then calculate the difference. A single big win does not guarantee a positive season NRR.

What about DLS, Super Overs and no-result matches?

NRR treatment depends on the event's playing conditions. The IPL's published rules count matches where a result is achieved, provide special credited runs and overs for DLS-affected matches, and use the full entitled quota when a side is all out. This tool does not calculate official DLS adjustments or NRR from Super Over scores. For those cases, follow the tournament's official standings or apply the event-specific adjusted figures before using any calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How is net run rate calculated in IPL?

Subtract the total runs conceded divided by the total overs bowled from the total runs scored divided by the total overs faced, using all qualifying regular-season matches and the tournament's all-out and rain-adjustment rules.

Does winning a match always increase NRR?

Not necessarily. The new NRR depends on how many runs and overs are added to the season totals. A narrow win may be insufficient to improve an existing high NRR.

Is 18.4 overs equal to 18.4 decimal overs?

No. It equals 18 overs plus four balls, which is 18.6667 overs in decimal form.

What happens if a team is all out before 20 overs?

The innings is charged with the full allotted 20 overs when the team would otherwise have been entitled to that quota.

Can I calculate NRR for ODI cricket?

Yes. Set the full overs quota to 50 for ordinary completed ODI matches. This calculator does not support DLS revisions or competitions with different quotas for different matches.

Are no-result matches included?

Generally, matches without a result are excluded from NRR calculations. Some rain-interrupted matches achieve a result and use special credited figures under the event rules; this calculator does not automatically handle them.
