What is compound interest?

Compound interest means earning interest on your original principal and on interest that has already been added to the balance. This is often described as interest on interest. Over longer periods, compounding can create a widening gap between the amount you contributed and the ending balance.

Compound-growth projections are useful for savings accounts, fixed-return illustrations and long-term planning. For investments such as stocks or funds, the rate entered should be treated as an assumption rather than a guaranteed return.

Compound interest formula

For a single lump sum, the standard formula is A = P(1 + r/n)nt, where A is the future value, P is the starting principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is the number of compounding periods per year and t is time in years.

Recurring contributions require an additional future-value calculation because each contribution is invested for a different length of time. This calculator projects each recurring contribution from its contribution date to the selected end date using the same rate and compounding assumption.

What affects compound growth?

Time

More time allows previously earned interest to remain in the balance and potentially earn additional interest.

Rate of return

A higher assumed rate increases projected growth, but real investment returns can vary and higher expected returns may involve higher risk.

Regular contributions

Consistent contributions can have a major effect because they continuously add new principal to the compounding base.

Monthly, daily and annual compounding

Compounding frequency describes how often interest is added to the balance. With the same nominal annual rate, more frequent compounding generally produces a slightly higher effective return because interest is credited more often.

  • Daily: 365 compounding periods per year.
  • Monthly: 12 periods per year.
  • Quarterly: 4 periods per year.
  • Semiannual: 2 periods per year.
  • Annual: 1 period per year.

Beginning vs end-of-period contributions

A contribution made at the beginning of a period has slightly more time to compound than the same contribution made at the end of the period. This difference becomes more visible over long time horizons or at higher assumed rates.

Compound interest vs simple interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus previously accumulated interest. Because of this, the difference between the two methods generally becomes larger as time increases.

Inflation, fees, taxes and investment risk

The calculator shows a mathematical projection based on the inputs you provide. It does not automatically reduce the result for inflation, taxes, account fees or investment losses. If you are modelling a real investment, those factors can materially change the amount you eventually keep.

Important: A fixed compound-interest calculation is not a forecast of stock-market returns. Market returns vary from year to year and can be negative. Use realistic assumptions and treat the result as a scenario, not a promise.

Common compound-interest mistakes

  • Using 8 instead of 0.08 in a manual formula: percentage rates must be converted to decimals.
  • Ignoring contribution timing: earlier contributions generally compound for longer.
  • Confusing nominal rate and APY/effective return: compounding frequency changes the effective annual result.
  • Assuming a constant investment return: real market returns fluctuate.
  • Ignoring inflation, taxes and fees: future purchasing power can be lower than the nominal balance.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is interest earned on the principal plus interest that has already accumulated.

What is the compound interest formula?

For a lump sum, A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), where P is principal, r is the annual rate, n is compounding frequency and t is time in years.

Do regular contributions compound too?

Yes. Once a contribution is added to the balance, it can participate in future growth according to the assumed rate and compounding schedule.

Is monthly compounding better than annual compounding?

With the same nominal annual rate, more frequent compounding generally results in a slightly higher effective return.

Is this an investment-return guarantee?

No. It is a mathematical projection using the rate you enter. Real investment returns may be higher or lower and can be negative.

Are my inputs stored?

The calculation runs in your browser. The entered values do not need to be sent to a server to produce the result.
